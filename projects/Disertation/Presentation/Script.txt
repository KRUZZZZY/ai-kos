Slide 3: The Erdős–Rényi Model (Definition)

Script:
"We start in 1959 with Erdős and Rényi. Their model is the 'blank slate' of graph theory. You take n points and flip a coin for every possible connection with a probability p. It’s mathematically elegant because it’s so simple, and it leads to a very predictable degree distribution—a Poisson curve where most nodes have roughly the same number of friends."  
Slide 4: ER Phase Transitions

Script:
"The most famous feature here is the Phase Transition. Think of it like water freezing. If your probability p is too low, the graph is just 'dust'—tiny isolated clusters. But once you hit a critical threshold, a 'Giant Component' suddenly emerges that swallows up most of the graph. This is the mathematical birth of a network."  
Slide 5: ER Simulation (RQ1 Results)

Script:
"When I ran the simulations for RQ1, I found something interesting. Theory says the transition should happen at p=0.001, but my data showed it closer to 0.0014. This 40% gap isn't a mistake; it's a 'finite-size effect'. Because my graph only had 1,000 nodes, the transition was a smooth ramp rather than the sharp, infinite step described in textbooks."  
Slide 6: Watts–Strogatz (The Small-World Problem)

Script:
"But Erdős–Rényi has a problem: it doesn't look like a social network. In real life, my friends are likely friends with each other—that’s 'clustering.' Watts and Strogatz realized that regular lattices have high clustering but huge distances, while random graphs have short distances but no clustering. They wanted to find the middle ground."  
Slide 7: WS Simulation (RQ2 Results)

Script:
"For RQ2, I looked for the 'Small-World Window.' By taking a circle of nodes and randomly 'rewiring' just a few edges, you get the best of both worlds. My simulations confirmed their theory: at a rewiring rate of 10−3, the path length drops like a stone, but the local clusters stay almost entirely intact. It’s the 'six degrees of separation' in action."  
Slide 8: Barabási–Albert (The Power Law)

Script:
"Finally, we have the Barabási–Albert model. If you look at the World Wide Web, it doesn't look like a Poisson curve. It has 'hubs'—nodes like Google or Amazon with millions of links. This is a Scale-Free network. The math tells us the degree distribution follows a Power Law (k−3), meaning hubs are rare but inevitable."  
Slide 9: Preferential Attachment (The Mechanism)

Script:
"How do these hubs form? Through Preferential Attachment, or the 'rich get richer' effect. Unlike the previous models where the number of nodes is fixed, this graph grows over time. New nodes prefer to link to existing nodes that are already popular. This feedback loop is what creates the massive disparity between a hub and a leaf."  
Slide 10: BA Simulation (RQ3 Results)

Script:

It's close to the theoretical −3, and the slight difference again comes down to those 'finite-size effects'—real-world simulations have limits that asymptotic theory doesn't."

Slide 11: The Story of Random Graphs (Timeline)

Script:
"Before we wrap up the data, I want to take a step back and look at the story of how we got here. Random graph theory didn't start with Python simulations; it began in 1938 with researchers like Jennings and Moreno looking at 'chance sociograms' to understand human links.  

We then moved into the era of pure mathematics in 1959 with the Erdős-Rényi model—this was abstract probability at its finest. It wasn't until the late 90s and early 2000s, with Watts, Strogatz, Barabási, and Albert, that the field shifted to model the 'Small-World' and 'Scale-Free' structures of the modern world.  

My own journey started in 2025, thanks to a prompt from Dr. Angelica Pachon, to see if these theoretical limits actually hold up when we build them from scratch. This timeline represents a shift from abstract definitions to something I could finally test, plot, and understand."
